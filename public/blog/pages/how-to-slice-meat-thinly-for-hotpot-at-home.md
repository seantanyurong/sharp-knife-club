Pre-sliced hotpot meat from the supermarket is convenient but expensive, and you rarely get to choose the cut. Slicing it yourself lets you pick better meat, save money, and serve slices that cook in seconds in the steamboat.

The good news is that you don't need a meat slicer. You need three things: the right temperature, the right technique, and a genuinely sharp knife.

###### Why Home-Sliced Meat Often Fails

Most first attempts end with thick, uneven slices that tear or bunch up. This usually comes down to:

- **Meat that is too soft.** Room-temperature or fridge-cold meat squishes under the knife.
- **A dull knife.** It crushes the fibres instead of cutting through them.
- **Sawing back and forth.** Each stroke tears the slice slightly.
- **Cutting with the grain.** This leaves long, chewy fibres.

Fix these four things and your slices will improve dramatically.

###### Step 1: Partially Freeze the Meat

This is the secret. Put the whole piece of meat in the freezer for **45 to 90 minutes**, depending on thickness. You want it firm on the outside and slightly yielding in the centre, not frozen solid.

Firm meat holds its shape under the knife, which makes thin slices possible.

**Do not try to slice fully frozen meat.** That's when knives chip and fingers get hurt. If it's rock-hard, leave it in the fridge for 20 to 30 minutes first.

###### Step 2: Choose the Right Cut

For beef:

- **Ribeye**: well marbled, tender, and classic for shabu-shabu
- **Chuck or short plate**: more fat, richer flavour, great for steamboat
- **Sirloin**: leaner, best sliced very thin

For pork:

- **Pork belly**: rich and popular
- **Pork collar (shoulder butt)**: well balanced fat and tenderness
- **Loin**: lean, needs very thin slices to stay tender

###### Step 3: Use a Long, Sharp Knife

A long blade lets you cut each slice in a single stroke. Good choices:

- **Chef knife or gyuto**, 21 cm or longer
- **Sujihiki or slicing knife**, if you have one
- **Vegetable cleaver**, which works well thanks to its weight and straight edge

The most important factor is sharpness. A sharp edge slices cleanly through firm meat. A dull edge slips on the surface or tears it.

###### Step 4: Slice Against the Grain, in One Stroke

1. **Find the grain.** Look for the lines of muscle fibre running through the meat.
2. **Position the knife across them**, at a right angle.
3. **Start at the heel and draw the knife toward you** in one long, smooth stroke, finishing at the tip.
4. **Let the blade do the work.** Use gentle downward pressure, and don't saw.
5. **Aim for 2 to 3 mm thickness.** Thinner is better for shabu-shabu.

Keep your guiding hand in a claw grip, with fingertips tucked away from the edge.

###### Step 5: Arrange and Chill

Lay slices on a cold plate in a single layer, slightly overlapping. Cover and refrigerate until the steamboat is ready. If you're slicing a lot, work in batches and keep the rest of the meat in the freezer so it stays firm.

###### Don't Forget the Fish

The same method works well for fish slices in fish soup or steamboat. Firm, chilled fillets and a sharp, long knife give you clean slices that hold together in the broth.

###### Food Safety Reminders

- Use a separate board for raw meat, or wash thoroughly between uses
- Keep sliced meat cold until it goes into the pot
- Use separate chopsticks for raw meat and for eating
- Wash and dry the knife promptly after slicing

###### Final Thoughts

Restaurant-thin hotpot slices aren't about expensive equipment. Partly freeze the meat, slice against the grain, and use one long stroke. Above all, use a knife sharp enough to glide through without crushing.

If your knife keeps slipping or tearing the meat, no amount of freezing will fix it. That's the sign it needs sharpening.
