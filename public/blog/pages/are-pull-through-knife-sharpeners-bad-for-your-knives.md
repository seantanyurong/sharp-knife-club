Pull-through sharpeners are the most popular sharpening tool in Singaporean kitchens. They are cheap, small, and easy to use: drag the knife through the slot a few times and it feels sharp again.

So are they ruining your knives? The honest answer is: **it depends on the sharpener, the knife, and how often you use it.** Some are fine for occasional touch-ups. Others take off steel quickly and leave a rough edge that dulls fast.

Here is how to tell which situation you are in.

###### How a Pull-Through Sharpener Works

Most pull-through sharpeners have two abrasive surfaces set in a V shape. As you pull the knife through, both sides of the edge are ground at once, at an angle fixed by the slot.

There are three common types:

- **Carbide slots**: two hard carbide blades that scrape steel off the edge
- **Ceramic or diamond wheels**: rotating abrasive wheels that grind the edge
- **Combination units**: a coarse slot and a fine slot side by side

They are convenient because they remove the hardest part of sharpening, which is holding a steady angle.

###### Where Pull-Through Sharpeners Go Wrong

**They scrape steel rather than refine it.** Carbide slots in particular tear steel off the edge. The knife feels sharp right away, but the edge is ragged and weak, so it dulls again within days.

**They remove more metal than necessary.** Many people pull the knife through "a few extra times to be safe." Over months, this wears the blade down visibly, especially near the heel.

**The angle may not match your knife.** Most units are set for Western knives at roughly 20 degrees per side. Many Japanese knives are sharpened closer to 15 degrees. Forcing a thin Japanese blade through a Western slot can blunt its geometry and sometimes chip the edge.

**They leave a recess near the heel.** Because the heel cannot sit in the slot the way the rest of the blade does, repeated use often wears a dip just in front of it. The edge then stops touching the board in that spot, and the knife "misses" when you chop.

###### Knives That Should Never Go in a Pull-Through

- Japanese knives with thin, hard edges
- Single-bevel knives such as deba, yanagiba, and usuba
- Ceramic knives (unless the sharpener is specifically designed for them)
- Serrated knives
- Expensive or sentimental knives you want to keep for decades

For these, a whetstone or professional sharpening is the safer route. See our guide on [Japanese vs Western sharpening methods](/blog/japanese-knife-sharpening-traditional-techniques-vs-western-methods) to learn why.

###### When a Pull-Through Sharpener Is Fine

There is a time and place for them. A pull-through can be reasonable when:

- The knife is an inexpensive, softer stainless knife
- You use the **fine** slot only, with light pressure
- You use it occasionally, not every time you cook
- You accept that the edge will not last as long as a whetstone edge

Used this way, it is closer to a quick touch-up than true sharpening.

###### How to Use One Without Wrecking Your Knife

If you do use one, these habits make a big difference:

1. **Start with the fine slot.** Only use the coarse slot if the knife is truly dull.
2. **Use very light pressure.** Let the abrasive do the work. Pressing harder removes more steel.
3. **Pull straight through, heel to tip.** Don't rock or tilt the handle.
4. **Stop at 3 to 5 pulls.** If it still isn't sharp, more pulls won't fix it.
5. **Wipe the blade afterwards** to remove metal filings before you cut food.

###### Signs a Pull-Through Has Already Damaged Your Knife

Check your knife for:

- **A visible dip or gap near the heel** when you rest the edge on a flat board
- **A wavy edge line** when you sight down the blade
- **Small chips** along a Japanese knife
- **Edges that go dull within days** even after you've sharpened them

Most of this can be corrected by reprofiling, which means resetting the edge so it is straight and even again. The longer it goes on, the more steel has to come off to fix it.

###### Better Alternatives

- **Honing rod** for weekly edge alignment on Western knives. See [honing vs sharpening](/blog/honing-vs-sharpening-whats-the-difference-and-when-to-do-each).
- **Whetstone** if you want to learn the skill. Start with [our beginner's guide to sharpening stones](/blog/choosing-the-best-knife-sharpening-stones-a-beginners-guide).
- **Professional sharpening** every few months to reset the edge properly.

###### Final Thoughts

Pull-through sharpeners are not evil, but they are often overused. Treat them as an occasional touch-up for inexpensive knives, not as a replacement for real sharpening. Keep your good knives well away from them.

If your knife always feels dull a few days after you "sharpen" it, the problem may be the sharpener, not the knife.
