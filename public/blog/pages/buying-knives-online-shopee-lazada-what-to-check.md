Online marketplaces have made it very easy to buy a "Japanese Damascus chef knife" for less than the price of a nice dinner. Some of these knives are great value. Many are not. Our sharpeners see both kinds every week.

The difficulty is that the listings look almost the same. Same dramatic photos, same five-star ratings, same claims about "VG-10" and "67 layers." Here's how to tell what you're actually buying before you check out.

###### 1. Be Sceptical of "Damascus" Patterns

True Damascus or layered steel is made by forge-welding layers of different steel together. It costs a lot to produce.

Many low-cost listings use a **laser-etched or printed pattern** on a plain steel blade. It looks the same in photos and wears off over time. That isn't necessarily a bad knife, but it isn't what the listing suggests.

**Warning signs:**

- Pattern looks perfectly identical across every knife in the listing
- Very low price for a "67-layer" or "VG-10 Damascus" knife
- No information about the core steel at all
- The pattern stops sharply at the edge bevel

If you're paying for the pattern, make sure it's real. If you're paying for performance, the steel underneath matters more.

###### 2. Look for a Named Steel, Not Just "High Carbon Stainless"

"High carbon stainless steel" can describe almost anything. A useful listing names the steel, for example:

- **German-style**: X50CrMoV15, 1.4116
- **Japanese stainless**: VG-10, AUS-8, AUS-10, SG2/R2
- **Carbon steel**: White #2 (Shirogami), Blue #2 (Aogami)

Named steel is not a guarantee, but vague descriptions are a warning sign. Our guide to [knife materials](/blog/a-comparison-of-the-different-materials-used-for-knives) explains what these names mean in practice.

###### 3. Check the Hardness (HRC), If It's Listed

Hardness is measured on the Rockwell C scale (HRC). As a rough guide:

- **54 to 57 HRC**: softer, tougher, easier to sharpen, dulls faster
- **58 to 61 HRC**: a good balance for most home cooks
- **62+ HRC**: holds an edge longer but is more prone to chipping

Very cheap knives claiming 62+ HRC deserve some scepticism. Hardness is achieved through careful heat treatment, which is one of the things budget makers tend to skip.

###### 4. Read the Low-Star Reviews First

Five-star reviews are usually posted right after unboxing, when every knife is sharp. The useful information lives in the one- to three-star reviews, especially those written weeks later.

Look for repeated mentions of:

- Rust appearing quickly
- Handles cracking or coming loose
- Edges chipping in normal use
- "Sharp out of the box but dull in a week"

Repeated complaints about the same problem mean something.

###### 5. Look Closely at the Handle Join

Zoom into product photos where the blade meets the handle. Gaps, glue residue, or uneven finishing here often signal rushed manufacturing. In Singapore's humidity, gaps also trap moisture and can lead to rust or a loose handle.

###### 6. Be Wary of Huge Knife Sets

A 15-piece block set for a low price usually means 15 mediocre knives. Most home cooks only need three: a chef knife, a paring knife, and a bread knife. Spending the same money on fewer, better knives is almost always the smarter choice.

###### 7. Plan for the First Sharpening

Here's something most listings won't tell you: many budget knives ship with a mediocre factory edge. It feels sharp on paper but has a thick, uneven bevel.

The good news is that a decent but inexpensive blade can often be improved a lot by proper sharpening. We covered this in [can a cheap knife be sharpened to perform like an expensive one?](/blog/can-a-cheap-knife-be-sharpened-to-perform-like-an-expensive-one).

###### Quick Checklist Before You Buy

- Is the steel named specifically?
- Is the price realistic for what's claimed?
- Do low-star reviews mention rust or chipping?
- Does the handle join look clean?
- Do you actually need every knife in the set?

###### Final Thoughts

Buying knives online isn't a bad idea. You just need to read the listing like a sceptic. Pay for good steel and a solid handle, not for patterns and piece counts. Then give the knife a proper edge, and an affordable knife can serve you well for years.
